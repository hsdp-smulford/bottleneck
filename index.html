High-rise evacuation · stairwell merging

Why the 51st floor never got in

Every landing below you feeds another floor into the same stairwell. When each door merges fairly, the top floor's slice of the stair halves at every landing. The wait itself grows in a straight line, because the stair keeps moving the whole time.

Each floor adds the slower of two costs. P/C is how long its people take to fit into the stairwell. t is how long one flight takes to walk. Like any pipeline, the building drains at the speed of its slowest stage. In a real high-rise P/C wins by a wide margin, so T ≈ N·P/C.

Exact for this model: N·max(P/C, t) + min(P/C, t). The extra term is one floor of the faster stage.

Your floor's slice of the stair

s(k) = C·α(1 − α)k−1

At a zipper merge (α = ½) this is C / 2k. It halves at every landing below you.

What the model assumes

  • Everyone starts at once, and every stairwell is lumped into one flow C. Two 44″ stairs at about 0.8 people/s each give C ≈ 1.6.
  • The simulation is a cell-transmission model (Daganzo, 1994–95) with one stair cell per floor. Each landing merges its floor into the stair at share α. A jammed flight holds up to 2.5·C·t people.
  • Door position moves α. Galea et al. (2008) saw the floor favored, roughly 60:40, when its door sits next to the incoming flight. Field studies average close to 50:50.
  • Left out: firefighters climbing against the flow, fatigue over 50 flights, people who leave late, and phased evacuation, which exists to prevent this exact pileup.

Sources: Galea, Sharp & Lawrence 2008 · Boyce et al. 2012 · BRANZ SR 251 · Pauls, via IAFSS
